Capstone

Hierarchical Feature Selection for U.S. Census Data

2025 to 2026
M.S. capstone · California State University, Fullerton

Given any dataset with one value per state, it finds which of roughly 528 American Community Survey (ACS) variables, and which families of variables, best explain it. The hard part is shape: about 528 candidate features but only 51 rows (50 states plus DC), and many Census variables are just restatements of population size.

How it evolved

It began as a system for relating census demographics to congressional voting outcomes, and I generalized it to accept any state- or county-level target dataset.

What it found

  • Lung cancer incidence: strong, stable signal; 200 recipes in 2022 averaged R² 0.939 (range 0.884 to 0.974).
  • Opioid overdose deaths: mean R² near zero, but the best recipes reached about 0.6: real but sparse signal.
  • UFO sightings (a stress test): best recipes fell from 0.80 in 2013 to 0.04 in 2016, which I traced to a change in how the source releases its data rather than to the method.
  • The cancer data are raw counts, so population size confounds them; I documented this and how to correct for it (age-adjusted rates, or dividing by ACS total population after the join).

Starting in 2016 I worked independently as a bioinformatics developer for academic genomics and ecology research groups. Each card is one project I contributed to; click one for details.

The question

AMD is a leading cause of vision loss in older adults, and mitochondrial damage in the retina is thought to play a part. Do low-frequency mitochondrial DNA variants (heteroplasmy) differ between blood, retina and the RPE+choroid layer, and do any track with AMD?

My part

Formal analysis of whole-mitochondrial-genome sequencing from donor tissue, plus 23 "hot-spot" variants across 222 blood samples.

Mentorship

This wasn't my first research project, but it was my first deep dive into genetics. Dr. M. Cristina Kenney mentored me through it, from what a genetics study demands to how mitochondrial inheritance and heteroplasmy work, and patiently answered every question I brought her as a largely self-taught geneticist.

What it showed

Blood carried far more heteroplasmy than eye tissue, suggesting the retina is relatively protected. Two variants (m.13095T>C, m.13105A>G) were more common in AMD, making them candidate biomarkers. Transitions outnumbered transversions, pointing to replication errors rather than oxidative damage.

Paper · PLOS ONE 2021
The question

Pediatric systemic lupus erythematosus is more severe in some ethnic groups. Mitochondrial DNA is inherited only from the mother, so its haplogroup records maternal ancestry. Does haplogroup track with disease activity, organ damage or lupus nephritis?

My part

First author. I co-conceived the study and its methods, curated the sequencing and clinical data for 25 patients, built the analysis software, validated the results against disease-activity (SLEDAI-2K) and organ-damage (SLICC) scores, and co-wrote the paper.

The hurdle

Sample size. Twenty-five patients across four maternal lineages, some with only four patients each, is far too few for standard population-genetics methods. The analysis instead used rank-based, nonparametric statistics suited to small, uneven groups: a Kruskal–Wallis test across all lineages with pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum follow-ups, exact tests for categorical outcomes, and organ-damage scores recast as damaged vs. not, since most patients scored zero. When the eligible cohort was revised late in the project, I rebuilt and re-validated the analysis on the new sample.

What it showed

Disease activity differed by maternal lineage (p = 0.017): it was highest in the Amerindian and African lineages, and significantly higher in Amerindian than in Caucasian or Asian patients. Lupus nephritis was most frequent and most severe in the Amerindian (85%) and African (80%) lineages, and organ damage trended the same way without reaching significance. The sample is small, but it suggests mitochondrial ancestry may contribute to disparities in severity.

What it is

A tool for mitochondrial DNA variant data, the same kind of data behind my research papers. You upload VCF files and it places every call on the rCRS reference, assigns each sample a PhyloTree 17 haplogroup, and lets you build an analysis as a graph: filters, comparisons between groups, germline and somatic classes by allele fraction, gnomAD and ClinVar annotation, mutation density along the genome with permutation tests, and patient-group statistics (Kruskal-Wallis, Wilcoxon, Fisher's exact, ANOVA, Spearman).

How I built it

It began as a layered Python app for a research collaboration: a versioned SQLite catalog, a standard-library web server, and clients for Ensembl VEP, ClinVar and MSeqDR. Research data can't be hosted publicly, so I rebuilt it as a Godot app compiled to WebAssembly that runs entirely in the browser: files are read locally, kept in the browser's storage, and saved as a workspace file. It uses columnar storage and chunked work, since the web build is single-threaded, and every push runs its tests and a public-data check before GitHub Actions deploys it.

How it holds up

Its haplogroup calls match HaploGrep 3.3.2 on all 2,534 people in the 1000 Genomes Project's mitochondrial data. From the data alone, it recreates 175 haplogroup-defining variants across 27 clades, every one a known PhyloTree marker. The patient-group tests follow Stata 18's defaults and are checked against published Stata results and scipy.

What it is

It started from the little figures on gym machines that light up the muscles an exercise works. Pick an exercise and BodySim shows its muscle engagement across a full 3D anatomy model, with each muscle's role (primary mover, synergist or stabilizer), relative involvement and confidence.

How I built it

A 960-structure musculoskeletal model, including 466 skeletal muscles, extracted from the open Z-Anatomy atlas through a Blender pipeline, cross-referenced to standard anatomical terminology, license-checked, and rendered in Godot at 60 FPS. Every exercise profile accounts for all 466 muscles, including explicit zeros.

Adding exercises

All 873 exercises from the open free-exercise-db are searchable. To make one visualizable, Claude drafts a definition from its instructions and photos: joint movements, stabilization demands and which side works. A deterministic tracer turns that into a profile of all 466 muscles using a weighted muscle-action table, so every result traces back to a rule. Each draft is frozen under a SHA-256 hash, and it goes live only when my vote and an independent Claude reviewer's both accept it. An auto mode runs drafting and review unattended within a token budget. 33 exercises are visualizable so far.

The reviewer is strict: it rejected about 30% of drafts. Its findings showed three things the tracer couldn't express (a load carried by one arm, joints that move but barely contribute, and exercises that are one-sided for the arms but two-sided for the legs), so I rebuilt the tracer to handle them.

The open problem

The goal is a weekly planner: give it goals like stronger chest and arms, 60-rep squat sets or ankle mobility, log reps to failure and fatigue, and get day-by-day plans. That needs a per-muscle load that adds up across exercises, and I haven't found research that supports such a number. Until I do, BodySim reports relative engagement with confidence levels rather than inventing one.
